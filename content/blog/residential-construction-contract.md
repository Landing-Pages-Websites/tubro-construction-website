## What a residential construction contract should name

Start with the parties and the property. The contract should name the homeowner, the contractor's legal business name, the job address, and the date. In Washington, a contractor who advertises or bids construction work must show a registration number. You can check that registration, the bond, and the insurance on file with the Department of Labor & Industries before you rely on the packet. Then read the scope as if you were explaining the job to a neighbor. A useful scope says which rooms change, which work is included, and which work is not. "Remodel the kitchen" is not a scope. A scope names demolition, framing, cabinets, counters, plumbing and electrical fixture locations, flooring, paint, and whether appliances are owner-supplied. The same standard applies to a bathroom, an addition, or a new home. If a line is vague, ask for it in writing before you sign.

Washington also requires a specific homeowner disclosure on many residential jobs of $1,000 or more, before work starts. State law, [RCW 18.27.114](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=18.27.114), calls for a Notice to Customer. It states the registration number, the bond or deposit on file, and the registration expiration date.

The notice warns that the bond may not cover a claim and that it is shared with other claimants. It also says you may withhold a stated retainage, and that unpaid suppliers or subcontractors may lien the property. You should receive it and sign that you received it. It is a disclosure, not a promise that a problem cannot happen.

## Price type, allowances, and what is excluded

The price section should say how the number was built. A fixed price covers a described scope. An allowance is a placeholder for an item you have not selected yet, such as tile, fixtures, or appliances. If the allowance is a guess, the contract should say what happens when the real selection costs more or less. Ask whether tax, permit fees, and dumpster or haul-off costs sit inside the price or outside it.

Exclusions matter as much as inclusions. A kitchen price that does not mention moving a sink, opening a wall, or replacing a subfloor can grow the day those conditions appear. Put owner-supplied items on their own list so nobody assumes the contractor is buying them. Tubro's public process is upfront pricing with no hidden fees and a free estimate. That is a pricing stance, not a promise that an allowance or an unseen condition stays at the first number. The contract is where that difference gets written down.

## Payment, schedule, and written change orders

Read the payment schedule against the work, not against a calendar alone. A fair schedule ties draws to stages you can see, such as demolition complete, rough-in inspected, cabinets set, or substantial completion. Ask what the deposit covers and whether any amount is held until the final walkthrough. RCW 18.27.114 tells homeowners they may withhold a contractually defined percentage as retainage for a stated period. If you want that protection, it has to be in the contract. A verbal plan to "hold some back" is not retainage.

The schedule should name a start condition and a completion target, plus what moves those dates. Permit review, long-lead cabinets, and selections you have not made are common reasons a date slips. A date with no start condition is a wish. Tie the schedule to the same scope you already read, and note who tells you when a date changes.

Read the draws in order before you initial the page. A deposit that is most of the price, with the rest due at the start, leaves you little leverage if the job stalls. Stage payments that follow visible work are easier to check on a walkthrough.

Ask which documents come with each draw, such as a short progress note or a list of work finished that week. If retainage is part of the deal, the contract should say the percentage, the period it is held, and the condition that releases it. Leaving that blank means there is no retainage, no matter what was said in the estimate meeting.

A change order is a written revision to scope, price, or time. It should say what changed, what it costs, and whether the completion date moves, and both sides should sign it before the extra work proceeds. Do not approve field changes by text and assume the contract caught up. Change orders deserve their own process. Here, the only question is whether this contract requires them in writing. ## Warranty, insurance, and the Washington notice

A warranty clause should say what the contractor stands behind, for how long, and how you start a claim. Workmanship and manufacturer warranties are not the same thing. A cabinet brand's warranty does not cover a loose install, and a workmanship warranty does not rewrite a product warranty. Ask what is excluded, including owner-supplied items and normal wear. Get the length and the contact path in the contract, not in a brochure.

Insurance and the bond are also easy to mix up. Washington requires registered contractors to be bonded and insured. The bond is a limited fund that can be claimed for certain losses. The Notice to Customer says it might not cover a claim. Other customers, suppliers, subcontractors, and taxing authorities can claim against that same bond.

Liability insurance is a separate filing. Hiring a registered contractor does not guarantee a trouble-free job. Labor & Industries says registration may provide protections that are not available otherwise. Its [hiring a contractor](https://lni.wa.gov/licensing-permits/contractors/hiring-a-contractor/) steps say to verify the registration, compare written bids, and get a written contract you understand.

Read the lien language with the same care. If a supplier or subcontractor is not paid, the property may be liened, and a homeowner can end up paying twice for the same work. The statute says you may request original lien-release documents from each supplier or subcontractor, and that the contractor must provide further information about those documents if you ask. Put the timing of those releases next to the payment schedule so a draw does not outrun the paperwork.

In the contract, look for the name or the role, how decisions are confirmed, and how a field question becomes a change order instead of a surprise.

Closeout is the last set of lines, and it is where rushed contracts go thin. A complete closeout names the final walkthrough, a written punch list, the time allowed to finish those items, and which manuals, warranties, and lien releases you receive before final payment. It should also say that final payment follows that list, not a verbal "we're done." If the job needs a permit, ask which inspections are required before that walkthrough. Requirements depend on the city and the scope, so the contract should not pretend one county rule covers every address in Western Washington.

1. Read the scope out loud and mark every line you cannot picture.

## FAQ: questions before you sign

### What is a residential construction contract?

It is the written agreement between you and the contractor for a home project. It should cover the scope, the price, the schedule, how changes are approved, and how the job is closed out. A verbal estimate is not that agreement.

### What should I review before I sign?

Review the scope and exclusions, allowances, the payment schedule, retainage, the start and completion terms, the change-order rule, warranty, insurance, the Washington Notice to Customer, the person you will contact, and the closeout list. If a line is missing, ask for it in the draft.

### Does Washington require a disclosure before work starts?

For many residential projects of $1,000 or more, RCW 18.27.114 requires a Notice to Customer before work starts. It discloses the registration number, the bond or deposit, and the expiration date, and it warns about bond limits, retainage, and liens. Signing it means you received the disclosure.

### Is a registered contractor's bond enough to cover my project?

Do not assume that. The required notice says the bond or deposit might not cover a claim and is not for your exclusive use. Verify the registration with Labor & Industries, and use the contract's payment and lien-release terms as your practical protection.
