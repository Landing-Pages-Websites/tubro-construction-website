## Phase-by-Phase Kitchen Remodel Timeline

A kitchen remodel moves through connected phases, and each phase depends on decisions or work completed before it. The exact sequence can vary with the scope, but a clear plan helps you understand what happens next and where timing can change.

1. **Estimate, design, and project planning.** The process begins by defining the goals, layout, and scope of the kitchen. A project may involve cabinetry, an island, plumbing fixtures, flooring, tile, countertops, appliances, or lighting, so the number of decisions affects the work that follows. Tubro Construction uses 20/20 design software to help homeowners visualize the finished project before construction begins. The process also includes a free estimate, design and material selection, and assignment of a project manager. Before work starts, confirm the approved plans, selections, responsibilities, and any applicable permit requirements.
2. **Material selection and ordering.** Cabinets, counters, fixtures, appliances, flooring, and tile need to be selected before the installation sequence can be finalized. The important dependency is not a generic number of days. It is whether the required materials are available, checked, and ready for the next trade. A change after ordering can affect measurements, fabrication, or installation planning. Your project manager can help coordinate these decisions and keep the plan visible.
3. **Protection and demolition.** Once the plan is ready, the work area is protected and the existing kitchen is removed as needed. Demolition may expose conditions that were not visible during planning, especially when walls, plumbing, electrical systems, or flooring are involved. The actual scope determines what must be removed and what can remain. This phase creates the clean starting point for the new kitchen.
4. **Rough-in work and inspections.** Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, framing, and other behind-the-wall work are addressed before surfaces are closed. Their locations must match the approved layout for cabinets, appliances, lighting, and fixtures. Where permits apply, inspections may be required at different points, and the number and timing depend on the jurisdiction and project scope. Do not assume walls can be closed until required rough work has been reviewed.
5. **Wall close-in, cabinetry, and measurements.** After rough work is accepted and the walls are ready, the project can move toward finished surfaces and cabinet installation. Cabinets establish the final locations for many fixtures and provide the base for accurate countertop measurements. This is why cabinet work generally needs to precede countertop templating. Any field condition or layout adjustment should be resolved before the next installation depends on it.
6. **Countertops and finish work.** Countertop templating follows cabinet installation, then fabrication and installation occur according to the selected material and project plan. Backsplash tile, flooring, paint, hardware, appliances, plumbing fixtures, and electrical finish work may follow in an order suited to the kitchen. These are connected tasks, not isolated appointments, so a change to one selection can affect another.
7. **Final inspections, cleanup, and punch list.** When affected trades are complete, any required final inspection can take place. The project then moves to cleanup and a walkthrough. A punch-list review gives you and the contractor a chance to identify remaining adjustments. Check the completed work, and confirm the project is ready for completion and warranty support.

## What Causes Kitchen Remodel Delays?

Several moving parts can affect how long a kitchen remodel takes. Tubro Construction describes most kitchen remodeling projects as taking anywhere from a few weeks to a few months, depending on the scope. That range reflects the difference between updating finishes and coordinating layout changes, cabinetry, plumbing, electrical work, flooring, tile, countertops, appliances, and lighting.

### Changes after work begins

Changing the layout, adding an island, moving plumbing, or revising cabinetry after construction starts can require new drawings, materials, or trade coordination. Even a small selection change may affect work that follows it. Confirming priorities and selections before demolition helps reduce avoidable disruption.

### Material and selection decisions

Cabinets, countertops, fixtures, appliances, and tile each need to fit the design and the surrounding work. A delayed decision or a material that does not arrive as expected can prevent the next trade from moving forward. Your project manager should keep these dependencies visible and identify decisions that need attention early.

### Hidden conditions and structural work

Opening walls or floors can reveal conditions that were not visible during planning, such as damaged materials, outdated wiring, plumbing concerns, or framing issues. Removing a wall or changing the structure can also add design, engineering, or approval requirements. These possibilities are not a reason to assume trouble, but they are why a precise schedule cannot be guaranteed before the existing conditions and final scope are understood.

### Trade coordination, permits, and inspections

Plumbing, electrical, HVAC, framing, cabinets, countertops, and finish work must be coordinated in an order that allows each trade to do its job. Permit and inspection requirements also depend on the jurisdiction and the scope. Government guidance from other cities shows that inspection counts and required permits can vary. So their timelines should not be treated as a promise for a project in King County or Pierce County. Rough work may need inspection before walls are closed, and final inspection generally follows completion of the affected trades. Clear communication with your contractor can help surface decisions and dependencies before they become schedule problems.

## How Can You Keep a Kitchen Remodel on Schedule?

Homeowners cannot control every condition behind a wall or every inspection requirement, but they can reduce avoidable pauses. The best place to start is with decisions. Finalize the layout, cabinetry, fixtures, finishes, appliances, and lighting with your contractor before construction begins whenever possible. If you are still comparing options during installation, work may need to pause while the plan changes.

### Confirm what the project includes

Ask for a clear scope of work and discuss which tasks depend on one another. For example, changes to plumbing or electrical systems may need to happen before walls, cabinets, or other finishes can be completed. Ask which selections need to be approved first, what materials must be available before installation, and whether permits or inspections could affect the sequence. These questions help you understand how long a kitchen remodel takes without relying on a generic calendar.

Before work starts, make a household plan. Decide where you will prepare meals, store food, wash dishes, and keep essential appliances. ### How long does the construction phase of a kitchen remodel take?

The construction phase varies because each kitchen has different demolition, rough-in, installation, and finish requirements. Demolition is followed by rough plumbing and electrical work when needed, then close-in work, cabinets, countertops, finishes, inspections, and a final punch-list review. ### Can you live in your house during a kitchen remodel?

Often, homeowners can remain in the house, but the decision depends on dust, noise, access, utility interruptions, and whether another safe food-preparation area is available. Discuss temporary kitchen needs, pathways, work hours, and protection for adjacent rooms before construction starts. Planning these details early helps your household make practical arrangements.
