<h2><strong>What to Ask a General Contractor Before Signing a Contract</strong></h2>




<h2><span>Introduction: Why the Questions Matter More Than the Price</span></h2>
<p><span><span>Most remodeling problems don’t start during construction—they start</span>
</span><strong>before the contract is signed</strong><span><span>. Homeowners focus heavily on price and timelines, but the biggest risks usually come from unclear scope, vague responsibility, and assumptions that were never written down. In</span>
</span><strong>Maple Valley</strong><span>, these issues are a common cause of budget overruns, delays, and disputes.</span></p><p><span><span>This guide walks through</span>
</span><strong>the specific questions homeowners should ask a general contractor before signing a contract</strong><span>, explains why each question matters, and shows what kind of answers indicate a well-run project versus a risky one.<br/></span></p>

<img alt="Brown-sided house with four white-framed windows; trees in the background and brown roof." src="/images/blog/legacy-6fa8c04e67a4d9f8.jpg" title="Brown-sided house with four white-framed windows; trees in the background and brown roof."/>



<h2><span>Before Anything Else: Clarify What You Are Actually Hiring Them To Do</span></h2>
<p><span><span>Before reviewing pricing or schedules, homeowners need clarity on</span>
</span><strong>the contractor’s role</strong><span>. A general contractor can range from a coordinator who subcontracts everything to a hands-on builder managing work directly.</span></p><p><span><span>﻿</span></span></p><p><span>At this stage, the goal is not to challenge the contractor, but to understand how responsibility is structured. Without that clarity, it’s impossible to evaluate the rest of the contract properly.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h2><span>Questions About Scope and Responsibilities</span></h2>
<p><span>The most important contract problems come from unclear scope. These questions help define what is included, excluded, and assumed.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h3><span>What exactly is included in the scope of work?</span></h3>
<p><span>This question should lead to a detailed explanation of what the contractor is responsible for delivering—not just broad descriptions like “kitchen remodel” or “bathroom renovation.”</span></p><p><span>A solid answer clearly defines:</span></p> <ul><li><span>What rooms and areas are included<br/></span></li><li><span>What systems are being touched<br/></span></li><li><span>What finishes, fixtures, and installations are covered<br/></span></li><li><span>What work is explicitly excluded<br/><br/></span></li></ul>
<p><span>If scope is vague, costs usually grow later.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h3><span>Who is responsible for design coordination and decisions?</span></h3>
<p><span>Some contractors work from homeowner-provided plans. Others manage design coordination themselves. This affects timelines, pricing, and accountability.</span></p><p><span>You want to know:</span></p> <ul><li><span>Who resolves design conflicts<br/></span></li><li><span>Who updates plans if conditions change<br/></span></li><li><span>How design decisions are documented<br/><br/></span></li></ul>
<p><span>Unclear design responsibility often leads to delays and rework.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h2><span>Questions About Pricing and Change Orders</span></h2>
<p><span>Price disputes are rarely about the base estimate—they’re about what happens when something changes.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h3><span>Is this a fixed-price contract or a cost-plus contract?</span></h3>
<p><span>The contract type determines how risk is shared.</span></p><p><span>A fixed-price contract defines a set cost for defined work, while a cost-plus contract charges actual costs plus a fee. Neither is automatically better, but the risks are different and must be understood before signing.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h3><span>How are change orders handled and priced?</span></h3>
<p><span>Change orders are normal in remodeling, but they should not be informal.</span></p><p><span>A reliable contractor can explain:</span></p> <ul><li><span>When a change order is required<br/></span></li><li><span>How pricing is calculated<br/></span></li><li><span>How approval is documented before work proceeds<br/><br/></span></li></ul>
<p><span>Verbal changes are a red flag.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h2><span>Questions About Timeline and Scheduling</span></h2>
<p><span>Timelines fail when they are treated as promises instead of plans.</span></p> <h3><span>What factors could realistically extend the timeline?</span></h3>
<p><span>An experienced contractor should openly discuss risks such as permitting delays, inspections, material lead times, or hidden conditions.</span></p><p><span>If a timeline sounds guaranteed with no caveats, it usually isn’t realistic.</span></p> <h3><span>How many projects will you be managing at the same time?</span></h3>
<p><span>This affects daily progress and communication. A contractor managing too many projects may struggle to maintain consistent scheduling.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h2><span>Questions About Permits, Inspections, and Compliance</span></h2>
<p><span>Permits are not optional details—they define legal and safety compliance.</span></p> <h3><span>Who pulls permits and schedules inspections?</span></h3>
<p><span>In most cases, the contractor should handle permits. This places responsibility for code compliance and inspections on the professional performing the work.</span></p><p><span>If the homeowner is asked to pull permits without a clear reason, ask why.</span></p> <h3><span>What happens if work fails an inspection?</span></h3>
<p><span>The answer should clearly assign responsibility for corrections and reinspection. Inspection failures should not become surprise costs.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h2><span>Questions About Subcontractors and Quality Control</span></h2>
<p><span>Most general contractors rely on subcontractors. That’s normal—but management matters.</span></p> <h3><span>Who are the subcontractors, and how long have you worked with them?</span></h3>
<p><span>Long-term working relationships usually lead to better coordination and accountability.</span></p> <h3><span>How is quality checked before moving to the next phase?</span></h3>
<p><span>Good contractors inspect work internally before calling for inspections or proceeding to finishes. This reduces rework and delays.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h2><span>Questions About Insurance, Licensing, and Risk</span></h2>
<p><span>These questions protect homeowners from liability and unfinished projects.</span></p> <h3><span>Are you licensed, bonded, and insured for this type of work?</span></h3>
<p><span>This should not be awkward to ask. Contractors should be able to provide proof.</span></p> <h3><span>What happens if the project is delayed or stopped?</span></h3>
<p><span>Life happens. The contract should explain how delays, disputes, or unexpected stoppages are handled.</span></p><p><br/></p> <h2><span>Decision Guide: How to Evaluate the Answers You Get</span></h2>
<p><span><span>The goal is not perfect answers—it’s</span>
</span><strong>clear, consistent answers</strong><span>.</span></p> <ol><li><strong>Specific answers signal preparation</strong><span><br/> Vague answers usually mean vague planning.<br/></span></li><li><strong>Written processes matter more than verbal reassurance</strong><span><br/> Good contractors document decisions.<br/></span></li><li><strong>Transparency about risk is a strength, not a weakness</strong><span><br/> Honest discussion of unknowns prevents surprises.<br/></span></li><li><strong>Responsibility should always be clear</strong><span><br/> If no one owns a task, the homeowner usually pays for it.<br/></span></li></ol>
<p><br/></p> <h2><span>Frequently Asked Questions</span></h2>
<ul><li><strong>Is the lowest bid usually the best choice?</strong><span><br/> Not always. Low bids often exclude necessary work or underestimate risk.</span></li><li><strong>Should I hire a contractor before design is complete?</strong><span><br/> Sometimes. Early involvement can improve budgeting, but roles must be clear.</span></li><li><strong>Is it normal to pay a deposit?</strong><span><br/> Yes, but payment schedules should align with progress—not just dates.</span></li><li><strong>Can contracts be changed after signing?</strong><span><br/> Yes, through documented change orders approved by both parties.</span></li><li><strong>What’s the biggest red flag before signing?</strong><span><br/> Unclear scope combined with pressure to sign quickly.</span></li></ul>

<p><span>A remodeling contract is not just paperwork—it is the roadmap for how decisions, money, and responsibility are handled. Asking the right questions before signing protects your budget, timeline, and peace of mind far more than negotiating price alone.</span></p><p><span><span>Working with a local general contractor like</span>
